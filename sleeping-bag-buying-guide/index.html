How to choose the right sleeping bag

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

For most campers, a down-insulated mummy bag with a comfort rating around 30°F (-1°C) offers the best warmth-to-weight ratio for three-season use, but synthetic fill is a smarter choice for damp climates. Focus on the comfort rating (not the extreme limit), choose a shape that matches your sleeping style, and don’t overlook packed size if you’re backpacking.

What temperature rating do you actually need?

Temperature ratings are the single most misunderstood spec. Bags are tested to an industry standard (EN/ISO) that gives three numbers: comfort, lower limit, and extreme. The comfort rating is what a “cold sleeper” woman would feel comfortable at; the lower limit is for a “warm sleeper” man. Most buyers should pick a bag whose comfort rating is about 10–15°F below the coldest night they expect. If you sleep cold, add another 5–10°F of buffer. Don’t chase the warmest bag you can find. A bag rated for sub-zero conditions will be heavy, bulky, and miserably hot for summer trips. Instead, think in layers: a 30°F comfort bag paired with a liner and warm base layers can cover shoulder seasons, while a summer bag and that same liner handles hot nights. Many seasoned campers own two bags – one for three-season use and one ultralight for summer.

Mummy, rectangular, or hybrid: which shape fits your style?

Mummy bags are tapered from shoulders to feet, cutting drafts and reducing weight and packed volume. For backpackers, the mummy is almost always the right choice because less air to heat means better efficiency. The trade-off is restricted legroom; if you roll around or sleep with bent knees, you might feel claustrophobic. Rectangular bags feel like a quilt – roomy and great for car camping or warm nights when you want to stick a leg out. They’re heavier and bigger when packed, and they lose heat faster. Semi-rectangular (or “spoon”) shapes split the difference, offering more elbow room than a mummy without the waste of a rectangle. Some brands now make women-specific bags with extra insulation in the core and a slightly shorter length.

Down vs. synthetic insulation: which is better for you?

Down (from geese or ducks) is lighter, compresses smaller, and lasts longer than synthetic – assuming it stays dry. Fill power (measured in cubic inches per ounce) indicates loft; 600–700 fill is good for car camping, 800+ for serious backpacking. The moment down gets wet, though, it clumps and loses nearly all insulating ability, and it takes forever to dry. Synthetic insulation (e.g., Polartec® Alpha, Climashield® Apex) performs well when damp, dries quickly, and costs much less. It’s heavier and bulkier for the same warmth, and it tends to lose loft after repeated compression. If you camp in consistently humid areas (Pacific Northwest, UK, Southeast Asia) or paddle trips where moisture is likely, go synthetic. For dry mountain climates or weight-conscious trips, down is hard to beat.

Weight and packed size: what’s reasonable?

A three-season down bag for backpacking typically weighs between 2 and 3.5 pounds and packs down to about the size of a cantaloupe. Synthetic bags in the same warmth class often weigh a pound more and pack to the size of a small watermelon. If you’re car camping, weight and size barely matter – you can bring a thick rectangular bag that weighs 4–5 pounds. Your budget and packing style should guide the trade-off. Ultralight down bags (under 2 pounds) can cost a good deal more but are worth every ounce for thru-hikers. For weekend trips, a well-reviewed synthetic bag in the 3–4 pound range is perfectly fine and much easier on the wallet. Always store your bag loose in a large cotton or mesh sack to preserve loft – never leave it compressed for weeks.

Shell fabric, zippers, and other features to look for

The shell fabric determines how water-resistant and breathable the bag is. Many down bags now use a DWR (durable water repellent) finish on the shell or even water-resistant down itself (treated with a hydrophobic coating). That helps but doesn’t make down waterproof – you still need a dry shelter and a dry stuff sack. Check the zipper: a full-length two-way zip lets you vent your feet or zip two bags together for couples. Draft tubes behind the zipper prevent heat loss, and a neck baffle or draft collar keeps warm air from escaping around your shoulders. Hoods with a drawcord are essential for cold nights – you can cinch them until only your nose shows. A small internal stash pocket is handy for glasses, phone, or lip balm.

How to choose the right size (and why length matters)

Sleeping bags are sized by height, usually in short, regular, and long. A bag that’s too long leaves dead air space that your body can’t heat, making you colder. A bag that’s too short compresses the foot box, reducing insulation loft and causing cold feet. Measure your height and match it to the manufacturer’s length guideline – many list a maximum user height. Women’s-specific bags are often shorter with a wider hip and narrower shoulder profile, plus extra insulation in the core areas. If you’re a woman of average height, a women’s bag will likely fit better and perform closer to its rated temperature than a unisex bag. For larger or taller people, look for “big” or “tall” sizes; some brands also offer “wide” versions that add girth without extra length.

Frequently asked questions

Can I wash my sleeping bag in a washing machine?

Yes, but use a front-loader (top-loaders with an agitator can damage baffles). Use a gentle cycle with a specialized down wash (for down) or mild soap (for synthetic), then dry on very low heat with clean tennis balls to break up clumps. Check the care tag – some bags are hang-dry only.

What’s the difference between comfort and lower limit ratings?

Comfort rating is the temperature at which a cold-sleeping woman can sleep comfortably; lower limit is the temperature at which a warm-sleeping man can sleep without shivering. For most people, the comfort rating is the number to use when choosing a bag for a given night.

Is down or synthetic better for wet weather camping?

Synthetic is better in consistently wet or humid conditions because it insulates when damp and dries quickly. Down loses loft when wet and takes a long time to dry, though water-resistant down treatments help somewhat. If you camp in rain forests or paddle trips, choose synthetic.

How should I store my sleeping bag when not in use?

Always store your bag loose in a large cotton or mesh storage sack (often called a “storage sack” or pillowcase) in a cool, dry place. Never keep it compressed in its stuff sack for more than a few days, as that permanently damages loft and reduces warmth.

Can two sleeping bags be zipped together?

Many bags with full-length two-way zippers that are “couple-compatible” can be zipped together if the zippers are on opposite sides (left-hand and right-hand). Check the product description for compatibility; some brands offer matching left- and right-zip models, or you can buy a couple’s quilt instead.

Do I need a sleeping bag liner?

A liner adds 5–10°F of warmth, keeps the bag cleaner, and can be used alone on hot nights. Silk or cotton liners are lightweight and comfortable; fleece liners add more warmth. If you only have a three-season bag, a liner is an inexpensive way to extend its range into colder weather.

In shortIf you remember one thing: let the comfort rating guide your choice, not the extreme number, and match the bag’s shape and insulation to the conditions you actually sleep in. A good bag is an investment in good nights outdoors, which means better days. Take care of it, and it will take care of you for many seasons.

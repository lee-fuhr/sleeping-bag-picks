Sleeping Bag questions answered

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

The best sleeping bag for you depends on your typical camping conditions, sleeping style, and budget. Prioritize a temperature rating that is a few degrees lower than the coldest night you expect, and choose a shape that matches your movement needs, mummy bags for warmth efficiency, rectangular for comfort, and semi-rectangular for a balance.

What temperature rating should I look for in a sleeping bag?

Temperature ratings are the single most important spec, but they can be misleading. Bags are tested to EN/ISO standards and given three numbers: comfort (for cold-sleeping women), lower limit (for average men), and extreme (survival only, not for comfort). Look for a comfort rating that is at least 10–15 degrees below the coldest temperature you plan to encounter. Keep in mind that ratings assume you are using a sleeping pad, sleeping in base layers, and that the bag is brand new. Older bags lose loft and effectiveness. If you sleep cold or use a tent with poor ventilation, subtract another 5–10 degrees from the comfort rating to be safe.

Down vs. synthetic insulation: which is better for me?

Down (from ducks or geese) offers the best warmth-to-weight ratio and packs down very small. It is also more breathable and lasts longer if cared for properly. But down loses nearly all insulating power when wet, takes ages to dry, and costs more. For ultralight backpacking or cold, dry climates, down is the clear winner. Synthetic insulation (hollow fibers like Polartec or PrimaLoft) performs well when damp, dries faster, and is significantly more affordable. It also retains more loft in humid conditions. The trade-offs: it is heavier and bulkier for the same warmth, and the fibers compress over time, reducing lifespan. Synthetic bags are ideal for wet-weather camping, budget trips, or for anyone who doesn’t want to baby their gear.

What sleeping bag shape should I choose?

Three main shapes dominate: mummy, rectangular, and semi-rectangular (sometimes called ‘barrel’ or ‘spoon’). Mummy bags taper tightly at the feet and have a hood that cinches around your face. They minimize dead air space, making them the warmest and lightest per degree of rating. However, they can feel restrictive, especially for side sleepers. Rectangular bags are like a quilt with a zipper, roomy, comfortable for tossing and turning, and easy to open fully for warm nights. The downside is that they are heavy, bulky, and less efficient at retaining heat. Semi-rectangular bags strike a middle ground: a bit more room in the shoulders and feet than a mummy but still tapering. They work well for campers who want some mobility without sacrificing too much warmth.

How do I know what size sleeping bag fits me?

Most sleeping bags come in standard lengths (regular and long), and a few offer short or tall versions. A bag that is too short will compress the foot insulation and leave your head exposed; one that is too long creates a cold air pocket at the feet. To find the right length, measure from the top of your head to the bottom of your heels while lying flat, then add about 6 inches for wiggle room. Beyond length, consider girth. Some brands offer wide cuts for larger people or those who sleep with extra layers. Women’s-specific bags are often shorter in length, narrower in the shoulders, and wider at the hips, plus they have a comfort rating that matches women’s generally lower metabolic rate. If you’re between sizes or like to sleep with gear inside, err on the slightly larger side.

How should I care for my sleeping bag?

Proper care dramatically extends a bag’s life. Store your sleeping bag loosely in a large cotton or mesh storage sack, never compressed in a stuff sack. For down bags, this preserves loft; for synthetic, it prevents fibers from creasing and losing shape. Avoid storing a bag wet or dirty, as grime clogs insulation. Wash a sleeping bag only when necessary, every couple of seasons or after a very dirty trip. Use a front-loading machine (top-loaders with agitators can rip seams) and a gentle, non-detergent cleaner specifically made for technical insulation. Dry it on low heat with a couple of clean tennis balls in the dryer to break up clumps. Air drying over a rack works too, but can take a full day.

Do I need a mummy hood or can I skip it?

A well-fitting hood can add 10 to 15 degrees of warmth to your sleep system by preventing heat loss from your head and neck. For winter camping or cold-weather trips, a hood is nearly essential. Many mummy bags have drawcords that let you tighten the hood to a small opening, keeping warm air in and cold drafts out. If you primarily camp in summer or in mild climates, a rectangular bag without a hood may be perfectly fine, you can always wear a beanie. Some semi-rectangular bags come with an optional draft collar but no full hood. Consider your typical conditions: if you camp below 50°F at night, look for a bag with an insulated hood and a draft tube along the zipper.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use two sleeping bags in cold weather?

Yes, layering a lighter summer bag inside a heavier winter bag can boost warmth, but it only works if the inner bag is loose enough to not compress the outer’s insulation. A better solution is often a sleeping bag liner made of fleece or silk, which adds 5–15 degrees without the bulk of a second bag.

What’s the difference between a comfort and extreme rating?

The comfort rating is the temperature at which a cold sleeper (typically a woman) can sleep in a relaxed position without feeling cold. The extreme rating means the bag will keep you alive, shivering, but alive, for six hours. Always plan around the comfort rating, never the extreme.

How do I dry a down sleeping bag after it gets wet?

Squeeze out excess water (don’t wring), then lay it flat on a towel and roll it up to absorb moisture. Air dry in a warm, ventilated area, turning it over every hour. A dryer on low heat with clean tennis balls can speed the process, but it may take several cycles. Never dry down on high heat.

Are women’s sleeping bags really different from men’s?

Yes, women’s bags are typically shorter in length, narrower at the shoulders, wider at the hips, and have extra insulation in the footbox and around the torso. They are also rated with a comfort temperature lower than the men’s version of the same model. If you are a woman of average height, a women’s bag will fit better and keep you warmer.

Can I use a sleeping bag without a sleeping pad?

Technically yes, but you will be very cold. The insulation underneath you gets compressed, losing most of its warmth. A sleeping pad provides crucial ground insulation, without one, you’ll lose heat into the ground even with a high-rated bag. Always pair a bag with a pad of appropriate R-value.

What is the best way to stuff a sleeping bag into its sack?

Start by turning the bag inside out and then stuffing it loosely into the sack, never roll it tightly, as that creates uneven compression. Many ultralight bags come with a compression sack; use that for packing but not for storage. Storing in a large cotton or mesh sack protects the loft.

In shortA sleeping bag is an investment in your sleep quality outdoors. Focus on matching the temperature rating to your climate, choose the insulation and shape that fit your comfort and mobility needs, and care for it properly to get years of use. No single bag works for every trip, but by asking these questions you can narrow down the one that will keep you warm, comfortable, and happy on the trail.
